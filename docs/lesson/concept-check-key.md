# Concept check: answer key (teacher)

**Giving it.** Hand out the same sheet before the unit and after it. Students work alone for 15 minutes, in the same conditions both times, with no game open and no notes. Team code and seat number pair the two sheets, so no name is needed. Score every item: questions 1 to 4 are 1 for correct and 0 otherwise; questions 5 to 8 are scored 0, 1 or 2 by the rubrics below. The total is out of 12. Report two numbers, before and after: the class mean, and the share of students who score 2 on question 5 (they correctly predict a gaming behaviour).

### Question 1

**Answer:** A

A bonus plan tells the person what counts as doing well, so they aim at what it measures. B is the most tempting wrong answer: a plan pays for the results it lists, not every task, and effort does not spread evenly. C and D fail because a plan changes what people choose, and it covers only what it names.

### Question 2

**Answer:** B

An AI told to earn as much bonus as it can aims at what the plan pays for, and whatever the plan leaves out can suffer. A is the most tempting wrong answer: the AI is given the plan, not the owners' wishes, and nothing makes it work out unstated goals. C and D describe an AI that acts without reference to the plan, which is the opposite of what it was told to do.

### Question 3

**Answer:** A

Skipping restocking and cutting staff lowers costs and lifts profit now, while long lines and empty shelves go unmeasured. B is the most tempting wrong answer: a plan paying for short waits and full shelves would push the manager to do the reverse. C would reward more staff hours, and D would reward good service, so neither explains the behaviour.

### Question 4

**Answer:** B

This is Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, people chase the number, and it stops showing what it was meant to show. A is the most tempting wrong answer, because it claims the opposite: attention on a target does not make the measure more accurate. C and D say nothing about people chasing the number.

### Question 5

Look for a gaming behaviour that the per-hour measure rewards.

- Score 2: Names a specific gamed behaviour (rushing or cutting calls short, transferring hard calls, hanging up, picking easy calls) and links it to the per-hour measure. Example: "Agents hang up on hard calls, because more short calls means more calls per hour."
- Score 1: Names a plausible bad behaviour but gives no link to the measure, or gives a link but only a vague behaviour. Example: "Agents will be rude to customers." Or: "They will go faster to get more calls."
- Score 0: Blank, off topic, or a wish with no gaming. Example: "Agents will work harder and customers will be happy."

### Question 6

Look for a person or group the plan ignores, and a harm that follows from it.

- Score 2: Names a stakeholder the plan leaves out (drivers, customers, other road users, kitchen staff) and a specific harm that follows. Example: "Drivers, who may speed or take risks to beat 30 minutes and could crash."
- Score 1: Names a stakeholder but gives no harm, or gives a vague harm. Example: "Drivers." Or: "Customers might be unhappy."
- Score 0: Blank, or names someone the plan already counts, with no harm. Example: "The owners, because they want faster delivery."

### Question 7

Score the two parts: a fix that addresses the flaw, and a plausible risk the fix still carries. A change that makes the flaw worse earns nothing for the fix.

- Score 2: Both parts. Changes or adds one measure that addresses the flaw (members still active after three months, member satisfaction) and names a real risk of the fix. Example: "Also pay on members still active after three months. Risk: staff could sign easy, loyal-looking people and skip others."
- Score 1: One of the two parts. Example: "Pay on retention" with no risk. Or: "Pay more per membership. Risk: staff might pressure customers." (no fix, a real risk).
- Score 0: Neither part, or blank. Example: "Pay more per membership."

### Question 8

Accept any answers that link a plausible goal, the measure, and a cheap way to meet the measure while missing the goal; opening accounts customers never asked for is one example, not the only one.

- Score 2: All three answered, and they fit together. Example: "They wanted real, lasting customers. Paid for the number of accounts opened. Cheapest: talk customers into accounts they will never use, or open accounts nobody asked for."
- Score 1: Two answered well, or all three answered but the cheapest way does not show the gap. Example: "Wanted growth. Paid for new accounts. Cheapest: work faster."
- Score 0: One or none answered well, or the answers only repeat the plan. Example: "Wanted accounts. Paid for accounts. Cheapest: opening accounts."
